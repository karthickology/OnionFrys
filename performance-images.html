Performance Optimization

Image Optimization

Usually the heaviest thing on the page — and the fastest win available.

On this page

  1. Choosing the right format
  2. Sizing images for how they're actually displayed
  3. Lazy loading done right
  4. Compression without visible quality loss

Choosing the right format

"WebP and AVIF typically produce smaller files than JPEG or PNG at equivalent visual quality, and both now have broad browser support. That means faster load times without sacrificing how your images look. If you're still serving JPEGs and PNGs across the board, you're likely shipping more bytes than you need to.

That said, PNG still has its place for images that need true transparency or sharp, flat graphics like logos. It handles those edge cases better than the newer formats. Use the right format for the right image, and your pages will be faster without compromising quality."

Sizing images for how they're actually displayed

"A source image larger than its largest displayed size is pure waste—it forces visitors to download unnecessary bytes, especially on mobile where data and speed matter most. The image may look fine, but the hidden cost is slower load times and frustrated users.

Responsive images with a srcset solve this by letting the browser choose an appropriately sized file for the visitor's actual viewport. A phone gets a smaller file, a desktop gets a larger one—no one downloads more than they need. Less waste, faster page, better experience for everyone."

Lazy loading done right

"Native loading="lazy" defers offscreen images until they're about to enter the viewport—exactly what you want for anything below the fold. It saves bandwidth and speeds up initial load by skipping images visitors haven't scrolled to yet. Simple to implement, big impact on performance.

But it should not be applied to the hero image or anything visible on first paint, since that would delay the very thing LCP measures. The largest visible element needs to load fast—lazy loading it defeats the purpose. Use lazy loading for the rest, and keep hero images eager and immediate."

Compression without visible quality loss

"Most images can be compressed well past their original export settings before quality loss becomes visible. The difference between a 500KB file and a 150KB version is often unnoticeable to the eye—but the load time improvement is huge. Don't just accept what your design tool spits out.

A rough target of under 200KB for most web images, checked visually after compression, catches the majority of savings without a perceptible difference. Run it through a compression tool, compare side-by-side, and if it still looks good, ship the smaller version. Your pages will thank you, and your visitors won't notice the difference—except in speed."

The easiest win on the list

Image weight is usually the single biggest, and single easiest, performance fix available on an existing site — before touching JavaScript or infrastructure, check images first.

Action checklist

  • ✓Convert hero and large images to WebP or AVIF where supported
  • ✓Add a srcset so browsers can choose an appropriately sized file
  • ✓Lazy-load offscreen images, but never the hero image
  • ✓Compress every image before publishing, targeting well under 200KB
  • ✓Recheck total image weight whenever new pages are added
