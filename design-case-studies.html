Case Studies

The format that turns a project into evidence you can actually reason with.

On this page

  1. Starting with the real problem
  2. Being honest about constraints
  3. Explaining the decision, not just the outcome
  4. Quantifying the result where possible

Starting with the real problem

"Redesigned the checkout flow" describes an activity, not a problem. Starting from the actual business or user problem — a drop-off point, a support-ticket pattern, a stated goal — gives the rest of the case study something to be an answer to.

Being honest about constraints

Timeline pressure, existing technical debt, and stakeholder pushback all shape a real design decision. Naming them plainly makes a case study more credible, not less — a decision made under no constraints at all is rarely instructive to a reviewer.

Explaining the decision, not just the outcome

The most useful part of a case study is usually the alternatives that were considered and why one was chosen over the others — that's the part that demonstrates judgment, more than the final screenshot does.

Quantifying the result where possible

A number (conversion lift, support ticket reduction, task completion time) is the strongest evidence available. Where a clean number isn't available, qualitative signals — user quotes, stakeholder feedback, a clear before/after comparison — still carry real weight.

The decision is the evidence

A case study that only shows the final screen is a portfolio piece. A case study that shows what was ruled out and why is evidence of how you think.

Action checklist

  • ✓State the real problem before describing any design activity
  • ✓Name the actual constraints the project was made under
  • ✓Include at least one alternative that was considered and rejected
  • ✓Report a quantified result wherever the data exists
  • ✓Add qualitative evidence when a clean number isn't available

These checklists are meant to be used, not just read. Reach out if you want a second pair of eyes on your setup.
